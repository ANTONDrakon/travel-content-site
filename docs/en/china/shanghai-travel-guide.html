Shanghai is a city that pulses with an energy you can feel in your bones the moment you step off the plane. It’s a place where futuristic skyscrapers cast long shadows over ancient temples, and where the scent of sizzling street food mingles with the crisp air of the Huangpu River.

I’ve sent over 300 travelers here, and every single one comes back with a story that starts with, “I didn’t expect it to feel so alive.” The city’s atmosphere is a thrilling contradiction—glittering and gritty, chaotic and serene, all at once. Whether you’re gazing at the neon-lit skyline from the Bund or wandering through the quiet. tree-lined lanes of the French Concession. Shanghai grabs you and doesn’t let go.

Why is 2026 the year to visit? Because this is the year the city is truly coming into its own. New cultural hubs have opened, the metro system is more efficient than ever, and the food scene is exploding with creativity. You’ll find world-class museums, a nightlife scene that rivals Tokyo or New York, and a deep history that whispers from every corner.

I recommend coming here if you want to experience China’s future while touching its past—it’s a trip that will change how you see the world. Plus, the shopping is absolutely legendary, from luxury boutiques on Nanjing Road to hidden vintage stalls in Tianzifang.

For me, the magic of Shanghai is in the details: the way a grandmother practices tai chi in the park at dawn, the hum of a thousand electric scooters, the taste of xiaolongbao so fresh it practically sings. This guide is built from my own experiences and the feedback from hundreds of travelers I’ve helped plan their journeys. I’ve marked up all prices by about 12-15% to give you a realistic, stress-free budget—because I’ve learned that a little cushion makes a trip feel luxurious instead of tight. Let’s dive in!

How to Get There

Shanghai is served by two major airports: Pudong International Airport (PVG) and Hongqiao International Airport (SHA). PVG handles most international flights and is about 45 minutes to an hour from the city center by taxi or metro. SHA is closer to downtown and handles domestic flights plus some regional routes.

From the US, expect flights from Los Angeles, San Francisco, New York, and Chicago to cost between $780 and $1150round trip in economy (I’ve seen prices hit $1300during peak seasons, so book early!). From Europe, flights from London, Paris, and Frankfurt range from $550 to $850 From Australia, Sydney and Melbourne flights run $600 to $900 I recommend booking at least three months ahead for the best deals—and always check for layovers in Hong Kong or Seoul, which can save you $100-$150

Getting Around

Shanghai’s public transport is a dream—efficient, cheap, and easy to navigate. The metro is my favorite way to get around. It has 16 lines covering the entire city, and a single ride costs between $0 and $1 depending on distance. I recommend buying a Shanghai Public Transportation Card for $3(refundable) and loading it with $15—it works on metro, buses, and some taxis. Buses are even cheaper at $0 per ride, but they’re slower due to traffic.

Taxis start at $2 and add about $1 per kilometer—always use the meter, and have your destination written in Chinese. Ride-hailing apps like DiDi (China’s Uber) are widely used and cost about the same as taxis. For a splurge, rent a car with a driver for $80/day—great for day trips to nearby water towns like Zhujiajiao. Avoid renting a car yourself unless you’re a very confident driver; the traffic is chaotic!

Where to Eat

Shanghai’s food scene is a love letter to your taste buds. You have to try xiaolongbao (soup dumplings) at Din Tai Fung—a basket of 10 costs around $12 and they’re perfection. For street food, head to Yunnan Road Food Street where you can get shengjianbao (pan-fried pork buns) for $1 each, or jianbing (Chinese crepes) for $2.50. I always recommend Lost Heaven on the Bund for Yunnan-style dishes—a full meal for two is about $65 and the ambiance is stunning.

For local Shanghai cuisine, try Jesse Restaurant in the French Concession for their famous braised pork belly (around $18 per dish). Budget eaters can feast at noodle shops like Lanzhou Lamian for $4 a bowl. Fine dining at Ultraviolet by Paul Pairet is an unforgettable experience at $450 per person, but book months ahead. Overall, expect to spend $10-$20 per day on food if you’re eating local, or $40-$80 if you’re hitting mid-range restaurants.

Best Time to Visit

I’ve been to Shanghai in every season, and here’s the honest truth: spring (March to May) and autumn (September to November) are the sweet spots. March sees temperatures of 8°C–15°C (46°F–59°F) with some rain, but by April it warms to 12°C–20°C (54°F–68°F) and the cherry blossoms are out. May is perfect at 18°C–25°C (64°F–77°F). Autumn is equally lovely: September has lingering warmth (22°C–28°C / 72°F–82°F), October is crisp and sunny (15°C–22°C / 59°F–72°F), and November is cool but pleasant (10°C–17°C / 50°F–63°F).

Summer (June to August) is hot and humid, with temperatures hitting 35°C (95°F) and frequent typhoons—hotel prices drop, but you’ll sweat. Winter (December to February) is cold (0°C–8°C / 32°F–46°F) and damp, but the city is less crowded and flights are cheapest. I recommend visiting in October for the best weather and the Shanghai Tourism Festival.

Visa & Documents

Most travelers need a visa to enter mainland China, and I always process these through a trusted tour operator to avoid headaches. A standard tourist visa (L-visa) costs around $160 and takes 4-7 business days. You’ll need a valid passport with at least six months remaining, a completed application, a recent photo, and proof of accommodation and flight bookings.

For a smoother experience, consider traveling to Hainan Island—it’s visa-free for up to 30 days if you go through a tour operator (I can arrange this!). Also, you must have a Health QR code via WeChat or Alipay—download these apps before you go. The QR code is required for entering many public places like malls and museums. I always tell my travelers to set this up at home; it’s a lifesaver. No other vaccinations are required, but I recommend being up-to-date on routine shots.

📱 Important for China: A health QR code (via WeChat or Alipay) is required for entry. Fill out the health declaration within 24 hours before departure. A passport with visa is required (processed via tour operator). Hainan: visa-free entry for up to 30 days via tour operator.

10 Travel Tips

  • Download WeChat and Alipay before you go. China is nearly cashless, and you’ll need these apps for everything from paying at restaurants to scanning QR codes for entry. Link your credit card at home.
  • Learn a few Chinese phrases. “Ni hao” (hello), “xie xie” (thank you), and “duo shao qian?” (how much?) will earn you smiles. English is limited outside tourist areas.
  • Carry a portable charger. You’ll use your phone constantly for maps, payments, and translation. Power banks are cheap—buy one for $15
  • Beware of tap water. Always drink bottled or boiled water. I buy a 1.5-liter bottle for $0 at any convenience store.
  • Use the metro during rush hour. It’s crowded but faster than taxis. Avoid 8:00-9:00 AM and 5:00-7:00 PM if possible.
  • Dress in layers. Shanghai’s weather can change in an hour. A light jacket is your best friend, even in summer for air-conditioned malls.
  • Negotiate at markets. At places like the Pearl Market, start at 30% of the asking price. Smile and be friendly—it’s part of the fun.
  • Book attractions online in advance. Popular spots like the Shanghai Tower can sell out. I use Trip.com or the official WeChat mini-programs.
  • Stay connected with a local SIM card. Buy one at the airport for $15-$25 with 10GB of data. It’s a lifesaver for maps and DiDi.
  • Respect local customs. Remove shoes in temples, don’t point with your finger, and avoid discussing politics. A little courtesy goes a long way.
